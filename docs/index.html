The question

Echoes (2026) is a deliberately hard benchmark for AI-generated music: 4,468 tracks from 12 generators (Suno v5, Udio, ElevenLabs, Stable Audio, MusicGen and others), each made to imitate one of 296 real songs from the Free Music Archive. Its baseline detector is a frozen 2-billion-parameter Wav2Vec2 XLS-R model with logistic regression on top, reaching 15.3% equal error rate (EER) in-domain and 22.3% on generators held out of training.

Can traditional machine learning, meaning hand-built features and gradient-boosted trees, compete?

Method

MP3 → 32 kHz mono→ low-pass 15 kHz→ 10 s segments, loudness-normalized→ features→ XGBoost
  • Fine spectrum: long-term average log power per 7.8 Hz bin, 0–15 kHz (FFT size 4096).
  • Spectral residual: that spectrum minus a smoothed copy, keeping only narrow peaks and dips. This is the strongest single feature set.
  • MFCC statistics (20 coefficients plus deltas) and a path signature of eight band energies over time, both secondary.

Protocol follows the paper: 10-second segments and segment-level EER, with one stricter change. Train and test are split by reference song, so a real song and all of its AI imitations always fall on the same side. Results are means over 5 random 80:20 splits.

Seen generators: about half the error, 11 of 12 better. Unseen generators: parity on average, with large wins on Suno, Brev, SongGen and AudioLDM and clear losses on DiffRhythm, MusicGen and AceStep. The features are sharp but generator-specific: they learn each architecture's fingerprint.

What gives them away

  • MusicGen leaves a comb: tall, evenly spaced spikes across the spectrum (regular at about 398 Hz), the classic trace of an upsampling or codec decoder.
  • AudioLDM and SongGen go flat above about 7–9 kHz with a sharp edge near 8.8 kHz, consistent with generating at a low internal sample rate.
  • ElevenLabs has a distinctive bump near 6 kHz.
  • Suno and Brev share the same ripple spacing (about 469 Hz) and cover for each other when either is held out, matching the Echoes paper's observation that the two are confused in attribution.
  • Mubert is closest to real music and hardest to catch, consistent with assembling tracks from human-made loops rather than synthesizing audio.

Which generator made it?

Detection asks whether a clip is AI. Attribution asks which AI made it. If the fingerprints above really belong to the generators, the same features should be able to tell the 12 generators apart, not just separate them from real music.

Setup

This mirrors the paper's attribution experiment: 12 classes, AI segments only, scored by segment accuracy and macro-F1. The paper fits logistic regression on frozen Wav2Vec2 XLS-R 2B embeddings. Here, the features, the 10 s segments and the XGBoost settings are the same as for detection, switched to multiclass. The split is again by reference song, so all 12 imitations of a song are on the same side. That is stricter than the paper's track-level split. The majority-class rate is 14.3% (15.1% in the paper).

The spectral residual alone beats the 2B-parameter baseline. With all features, 10 of the 12 generators are named correctly at least 94.5% of the time, and MusicGen, AudioLDM and SongGen at over 99%.

Where it fails

  • Suno and Brev are mistaken for each other about 24% of the time in each direction. This is almost all of the remaining error. They share the same ~469 Hz ripple in the residual spectrum (above), and the Echoes paper reports the same pair at 26–27% with its own model. Two independent methods hitting the same confusion suggests the two services share much of their generation pipeline.
  • Mubert is named correctly 75% of the time. Its misses are spread across Stable Audio, Brev, Udio and ElevenLabs rather than concentrated on one rival. It is also the hardest generator to detect.

Is it the model, or the encoding?

Re-encoding every track at 128 kbps equalizes the final bitrate but not the history. Real songs arrive at 256–320 kbps, most generators at 128 kbps, ElevenLabs at 192 kbps and MusicGen near-uncompressed, and each is then re-encoded once more. Repeated MP3 compression leaves traces in exactly the fine spectral detail these features read. So a “which model” classifier could partly be learning “which encoding history”.

The control: before re-encoding, each track is trimmed by a random 144–432 samples (modulo the 576-sample MP3 granule), so the new encoder's frame grid cannot line up with any earlier one. The pass rule was fixed before running: the result stands only if it beats the paper on both metrics and drops by less than 2 points under the shift.

Accuracy dropped by 0.4 points. ElevenLabs and MusicGen, the two with the most unusual encoding histories, did not drop at all (97.4% to 97.7%, and 99.8% unchanged). The fingerprints belong to the generators.

This fits the explanation by Afchar et al. (ISMIR 2025): the upsampling layers in generative audio models leave small spectral peaks whose positions are set by the architecture, not by training data or learned weights. Different architectures give different peak patterns, which is what makes them separable here.

Caveats. Attribution is closed-set: it assumes the clip comes from one of the 12 generators. Echoes holds a single version of each service (for example Suno v5, Brev v4.5 Pro, ElevenLabs v2), so a fingerprint identifies that version, and whether it carries over to later releases is untested.

The encoding confound

Echoes does not ship the real audio; it is rebuilt from the Free Music Archive. The two sides arrive encoded very differently:

SourceTypical MP3 bitrate
Real (FMA)256–320 kbps (median ~306)
Most generators128 kbps
ElevenLabs192 kbps
MusicGen~512 kbps (near-uncompressed)

File bitrate alone separates real from AI with AUC 0.84. Any detector that sees high frequencies can partly learn “high bitrate = real.” So every track, real and AI, was re-encoded through the same MP3 encoder at the same settings, and the whole pipeline rerun:

Encoding accounted for 1–2 points. The rest is the generators. Anyone benchmarking on Echoes with real audio rebuilt from FMA should equalize encoding first.

Limitations

  • Unseen generators remain hard: parity with the baseline, not a win. The features capture architecture-specific fingerprints.
  • Mubert is barely detectable by either approach.
  • One dataset, with 296 real songs; test splits contain about 60 real songs each, hence the 5-split averages.
  • Our split by reference song is slightly stricter than the paper's track-level split, so comparisons are, if anything, conservative.
  • Spectral fingerprints may fade as generators improve their decoders or deliberately suppress artifacts.

Reproduce

Everything runs on a single workstation with Python and uv. The Echoes AI tracks come from Hugging Face. The 296 real tracks are matched to FMA by title and artist (296/296 exact) and pulled out of the 879 GB fma_full.zip with HTTP range requests, about 2.4 GB in total. Full commands are in the repository README.
